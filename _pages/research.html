My research lies at the intersection of discrete mathematics, algorithms, and evolutionary biology. The common thread is developing rigorous mathematical and computational tools for phylogenetic networks — graphs that biologists use to represent evolutionary histories involving events like hybridization and horizontal gene transfer.

New to phylogenetics? Read a plain-language introduction.

Biologists want to understand how species are related — which ones share a common ancestor and when lineages diverged from each other. The standard tool is a phylogenetic tree: a branching diagram, much like a family tree, where lineages only ever split apart over time.

But real evolution is messier. Sometimes two diverging lineages hybridize and produce offspring together — think of mules (horse × donkey), or the many plant species that formed through hybridization. Bacteria routinely swap genes with unrelated organisms. Two lineages can separate, evolve independently for millions of years, and then come back into contact and merge. None of these events can be represented by a simple tree.

A phylogenetic network is a generalization of a tree that allows these "reticulate" (merging) events, drawn as extra crossing edges in the graph. The central questions of my research are: can we reconstruct this network from genomic data collected from present-day species? Under what conditions is this theoretically possible? And how do we do it efficiently, even on large datasets?
