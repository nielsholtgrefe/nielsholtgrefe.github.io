My research lies at the intersection of discrete mathematics, algorithms, and evolutionary biology. The common thread is developing rigorous mathematical and computational tools for phylogenetic networks — graphs that biologists use to represent evolutionary histories involving events like hybridization and horizontal gene transfer.

New to phylogenetics? Start with a two-minute, plain-language introduction.

Every species alive today is the latest twig on an enormous family tree of life. Biologists want to read that history: which species share a common ancestor, and when their lineages first split apart. The classic picture is a phylogenetic tree — a branching "family tree of species" in which lineages only ever divide as time moves forward.

Why a tree is sometimes not enough

Real evolution is messier than pure branching. Sometimes two separate lineages come back together: horses and donkeys produce mules, many plants arise by hybridizing two parent species, and bacteria routinely swap genes with unrelated microbes. These "merging" (reticulate) events simply cannot be drawn on a tree. A phylogenetic network generalizes the tree by allowing such merges, shown as extra connecting edges.

Why do people study this?

Getting the picture right changes the conclusions we draw — about how a species came to be, where a useful trait (say, disease resistance) originally came from, or how quickly life diversified. Networks are increasingly needed for groups where hybridization and gene flow are common, from crops and fish to viruses.

The main questions I work on

Can we reconstruct the network of a group's shared history from genetic data of species living today? Is it even possible in principle, or can genuinely different histories leave identical traces in the data? And can we do it efficiently, even for hundreds of species? These threads run through the themes below.
